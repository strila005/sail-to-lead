1. General concept and methodology of sailing instruction

Purpose and educational value

Sailing is an environmentally clean water activity in which the wind is the only engine of the boat and the main steering tool is the crew's decisions and actions. For a Plast leader, a sailing boat is an ideal environment for "learning by doing" (learning-by-doing), which builds:

  • Responsibility and independence: in a single-handed boat (for example, the Optimist or Laser class) a young person makes decisions independently in changing wind and water conditions and is responsible for their own equipment and safety.

  • Teamwork and leadership: in a two-person boat (a dinghy) the crew is divided into helmsman and crew (sheet-hand). The helmsman takes the lead (assesses the situation, makes decisions, gives clear and timely commands), and the crew makes sure they are carried out (handles the front sail — the jib, adjusts the daggerboard and helps keep the boat balanced).

  • Discipline and respect for nature and for others: going out on the water requires strict observance of signals and distances and an understanding of the forces of nature.

Learning progression (Progression Structure)

Instruction is built on the principle "from simple on the shore to complex on the water", taking into account the age, physical fitness and previous experience of the participants:

  1. Stage 1. Introduction on the shore (theory + practice without water): the structure of a sailing boat, safety rules, putting on equipment, rigging the boat, determining wind direction and simulating steering on a trainer.

  2. Stage 2. First outing on the water in simple conditions: sailing in light or moderate wind (force 2–4 on the Beaufort scale) in a sheltered water area, sailing the basic beam reach (beam reach) between two buoys, getting a feel for the helm and balance.

  3. Stage 3. Manoeuvring and changing courses: mastering all points of sail relative to the wind (close-hauled, beam reach, broad reach, running), zigzagging against the wind (tacking upwind), carrying out a safe turn with the bow through the wind (tack).

  4. Stage 4. Complex manoeuvres and emergency training: carrying out a turn with the stern through the wind (gybe) and controlled practice of what to do when the boat capsizes (capsize recovery and turtling).

Distribution of responsibility: leader vs instructor

  • Plast leader: is responsible for the overall organisation of the group, educational support, distributing roles, running shore sessions (rigging the boat, learning the points of sail, practising turns on a trainer), psychological support for beginners, inclusive adaptations, monitoring environmental rules and running the final reflection.

  • Certified instructor: is responsible for the technical condition of the boats, assessing weather conditions (wind force on the Beaufort scale, waves, currents), clearance to go on the water, duty on the rescue motorboat, direct teaching of steering technique on the water and safety back-up during capsize practice.

2. Sailing session scenarios (Session Outlines)

Scenario 1. "Rig the boat and catch the wind" (Introductory session on the shore)

  • Educational goal: to build responsibility for preparing equipment, teach working in small groups, tell the parts of a sailing boat apart and determine the wind direction.

  • Who it is for: young people (11–17 years old) without previous experience; can be held at meetings by a water body or as a briefing at camp.

  • Prior skills: none required.

  • Format and duration: an interactive talk + a hands-on workshop on the shore; 60–90 minutes.

  • Materials and equipment: a sailing boat on the shore or on a trolley (Optimist for younger children/beginners or Laser / a training dinghy for older ones), diagrams of the boat's structure, a flag or ribbon as a wind indicator, life jackets and suitable water footwear, 3 stools, a stick (to imitate the tiller) and a rope (to imitate the sheet) for the trainer.

  • Course of the session:

    1. Introduction and safety briefing (15 min): getting to know the equipment. Each participant chooses and correctly fastens a life jacket and checks their footwear (closed toes, non-slip sole).

    2. Workshop "Rig the sailing boat" (30 min): in groups of 3–4, following a diagram, participants set up the mast, boom and sail (on an Optimist also the diagonal sprit), run the sheet through the blocks and prepare the daggerboard and the rudder with the tiller. They carry out a check "Is the boat ready to sail?" (is there any water in the hull, are all parts of the boat secured).

    3. Game "Where is the wind blowing and what is our course?" (20 min): the leader shows the wind direction with a flag. Participants turn the boat on the shore (or take up the boat's position themselves) and learn to identify: head to wind (the bow directly into the wind — the "no-go zone" 0∘, the boat does not move), close-hauled (sharply into the wind, angle up to 90∘), beam reach (the wind clearly from the side, 90∘), broad reach (the wind from behind and to the side, 90∘–180∘) and running (the wind straight at the back/stern, 180∘). They practise the commands "Head up!" (turn closer to the wind) and "Bear away!" (turn away from the wind).

    4. "Dry" tacking trainer (15 min): sitting on stools, participants practise hand coordination (changing hands on the tiller and sheet) and a smooth move from side to side during a tack.

  • Roles: the leader moderates the tasks and makes sure everyone is involved; the instructor checks that the rigging is correct. Participants share roles in the group: in charge of the hull/daggerboard, in charge of the mast/sail, safety checker.

  • Adaptations (Inclusion): for participants with hearing impairments we back up commands with visual gestures (a raised hand — attention/help); for participants who find it hard to concentrate we use colour-coding of the parts of the boat (for example, left/right side, pull in/ease out).

  • Reflection questions: What was the hardest part of rigging the boat? How did sharing roles help you cope faster? Why can't a boat sail straight into the wind?

Scenario 2. "First outing under sail: beam reach and tacking" (Basic practice on the water)

  • Educational goal: to develop self-confidence, team communication on the water ("helmsman — crew") and the ability to make joint decisions.

  • Who it is for: young people (11–17 years old) who have had the shore briefing.

  • Prior skills: the ability to put on a life jacket, knowledge of the basic parts of the boat (tiller, sheet, daggerboard, boom) and an understanding of the wind direction.

  • Format and duration: a practical session on the water; 90–120 minutes.

  • Materials and equipment: single-handed boats (Optimist for younger beginners, Laser for older ones) or two-person training dinghies, 2 training buoys, life jackets, wetsuits/clothing to suit the weather, whistles, the instructor's motor support boat.

  • Course of the session:

    1. Pre-start check (15 min): checking the wind (it should be light or moderate — force 2–4 on the Beaufort scale, 1.6–7.9 m/s). A reminder of the rules: do not forget to lower the daggerboard after leaving the shore (otherwise the boat will be carried sideways — it will drift).

    2. Exercise "Shuttle between the buoys" (40 min): the instructor sets out two buoys at right angles to the wind. Participants sail from buoy to buoy on the simplest and most stable course — beam reach (wind from the side). They learn to set the sail correctly: pull in (trim) the sheet until the sail stops flapping and the indicator ribbons ("telltales") lie parallel, and let it out (ease) if the wind is stronger. Participants watch the balance of the hull to avoid heavy heeling.

    3. Exercise "Tacking" (35 min): near the buoys participants change tack (take the bow of the boat through the wind line). In a two-person boat they practise a clear exchange: the helmsman looks over the water and says "Ready about!", the crew checks the space and replies "Ready!", the helmsman commands "Helm's a-lee!" and smoothly pushes the tiller away, both duck under the boom and cross in sync to the opposite side.

    4. Exercise "Stopping" (10 min): participants learn to stop the boat in a safe way — by bringing it head to wind (head to wind) or by letting the sheet out fully so that the sail spills the wind.

  • Roles: the instructor operates the support motorboat, corrects mistakes and provides back-up; the leader coordinates the change of crews on the shore, keeps track of time and participants' wellbeing and supports those who feel nervous.

  • Adaptations (Inclusion): participants with a higher level of anxiety or less physical strength are better off working in a pair with a more experienced partner or the instructor in a two-person boat, starting in the role of crew.

  • Reflection questions: How did you feel when the sail "caught" the wind? What helped your crew avoid colliding and make the tack on time? What emotions came up when the boat heeled over?

Scenario 3. "Tacking upwind and action in an emergency (capsize recovery)" (Advanced module)

  • Educational goal: to teach how to overcome obstacles (sailing upwind in a zigzag), act without panic in an extreme situation on the water and help team-mates.

  • Who it is for: young people (12–17 years old) who can already hold a beam reach confidently and carry out a tack.

  • Prior skills: confident swimming in a life jacket, basic handling of the tiller and sheet.

  • Format and duration: practice on the water under close supervision of the instructor; 90–120 minutes.

  • Materials and equipment: dinghies (Optimist, Laser or two-person), 3 buoys (a triangular course), life jackets, wetsuits (compulsory in cool water), the instructor's rescue boat.

  • Course of the session:

    1. Exercise "Triangle of courses and tacking upwind" (45 min): participants sail a route on all points of sail: they work upwind in a zigzag (tacking upwind on a close-hauled course, changing tacks through a tack with the daggerboard fully down), then sail across the wind (beam reach, daggerboard halfway), and return on a full downwind course (broad reach/running, with the daggerboard raised to avoid a sudden capsize). If needed, under the instructor's supervision, they practise a careful gybe (first pulling the sheet in to the centre so that the boom does not hit anyone on the head).

    2. Exercise "Capsize simulation" (45 min): in shallow water or near the instructor's boat participants deliberately tip the boat onto its side (capsize) and practise a clear rescue routine:

      • do not panic — the boat will not sink (it has buoyancy tanks or a sealed hull);

      • swim round to the bottom side, where the daggerboard sticks out;

      • pull on the daggerboard (on an Optimist) or stand on it and use your own weight to right the boat (on a Laser);

      • climb into the cockpit, check that the rudder, daggerboard and mast are in place, bail out the water and carry on sailing.

      • Extra (for older participants): going through what to do when the boat has capsized completely and is upside down (turtling).

  • Roles: the instructor stays right next to each boat during the capsize simulation; the leader records successes, reminds everyone about mutual help on the water and runs the debrief.

  • Adaptations (Inclusion): the capsize simulation is strictly voluntary; if a participant is afraid of cold water or immersion, they can first watch from the support boat or practise righting the boat in shallow water, waist-deep.

  • Reflection questions: What helped you stay calm when the boat capsized? How can we apply the principle of "tacking" (moving towards a goal in a zigzag around obstacles) in life and in a Plast group?

3. Adapting sessions to the type of water body

The same scenario cannot be mechanically moved from one location to another — the leader and the instructor must take into account the type of water body:

Criterion Lake / reservoir River Sea / large bay
Main features No current, moderate waves, but the wind can swing sharply because of woods or hills on the shore. There is a current, a narrow stretch of water, possibly heavy traffic of other vessels, shoals near the banks. Stronger winds, waves, tides, salinity of the water, a large expanse.
Changes to the scenarios Best for beginners' start. Short distances between buoys can be set. Remind participants to watch for changes in wind direction. A narrow channel forces frequent tacks when tacking upwind. Paddling out of the harbour to a wider place. Always take account of where the current carries you (so that you are not swept onto obstacles or a bridge). "Capsize simulation" must be practised before going out on open water. A stricter limit on wind force (for beginners no more than Beaufort 3–4). The presence of a rescue boat is critical.

Card 6. Austria (AT)

  1. Country and region: Austria, the federal state of Burgenland — Lake Neusiedl (Neusiedler See).

  2. Location name, water body and address: Segelschule Hofbauer / Wassersportzentrum Neusiedl am See (or the youth sailing base Segelschule Podersdorf / Rust); water body: the steppe lake Neusiedler See (the largest lake in Austria, with an average depth of only 1–1.5 m!); address: Seegelände 1, 7100 Neusiedl am See, Burgenland, Österreich.

  3. Operator / partner and public contact:

    • Segelschule Hofbauer (Neusiedl am See) — website: segelschule-hofbauer.at, email: office@segelschule-hofbauer.at, tel.: +43 2167 2325 (or the Österreichischer Segel-Verband - OeSV, segelverband.at).

    • Scout partner in Austria: Pfadfinder und Pfadfinderinnen Österreichs (PPÖ) — ppoe.at.

  4. Matching scenario and type of water body: a unique large shallow lake (Neusiedler See — depth 1–1.5 m across almost the whole water area, muddy-sandy bottom, no current, steady winds). Thanks to the shallow depth it is psychologically the safest for beginners — ideal for Scenarios 1–3 (even if the boat capsizes, in most places a participant can simply stand on the bottom, but because of the muddy bottom it is forbidden to jump from the boat head first!).

  5. Equipment and fleet: the school has children's and youth Optimist, Laser / ILCA, two-person dinghies RS Feva / Topaz / 420 and stable group keel/dinghy boats for beginners (Jollen). Life jackets and wetsuits are provided on site; closed neoprene or sports water footwear is compulsory (to protect the feet on the bottom).

  6. Instructors and safety: certified instructors of the OeSV / VÖWS (Verband Österreichischer Windsurfing- & Segelschulen); the school's rescue boats and the lake rescue service Wasserrettung Burgenland are on duty.
