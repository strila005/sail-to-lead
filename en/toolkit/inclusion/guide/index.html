1. Purpose and core principles of support

The purpose of this section is to help Plast leaders and youth leaders create an accessible, inclusive and psychologically safe environment for young people to take part in water activities, including sailing and windsurfing.

Special attention is given to young people with fewer opportunities, who may face economic, social, language, geographical, physical or psychological barriers.

Such participants may include young people from low-income families, rural and remote communities, young people with disabilities, displaced people, refugees and people who have lived through war.

Core principle: equal opportunities do not mean identical conditions for everyone. Every participant must be able to join the learning in a way that is accessible, safe and meaningful for them.

To achieve this, organisers should:

  • Identify possible barriers before the activity.
  • Offer adapted forms of participation without stigmatisation.
  • Ensure access to the necessary equipment and information.
  • Take into account each person's individual learning pace.
  • Support the participant's right to express discomfort, ask for help or decline a particular task.
  • Create conditions for cooperation, mutual respect and reflection.

2. Main barriers to participation and how to overcome them

Financial barriers

Taking part in water activities may require spending on transport, accommodation, food, equipment rental, instructor services and insurance. For young people with limited financial resources, such costs can be a reason not to take part.

Possible solutions:

  • Organising free or subsidised sessions with the support of youth organisations, local communities and sports clubs.
  • Using shared equipment provided by clubs or partners.
  • Looking for grant programmes for youth and sports initiatives.
  • Creating a shared budget to cover the costs of participants who need support.
  • Informing everyone in advance about all compulsory and possible additional costs.

Practical example: a local scout group agrees with a sailing club on a group session using the club's equipment. Participants do not need to buy life jackets or pay for individual boat rental themselves.

Before the activity, confirm the price, the number of places available, what the service includes and the conditions for using the equipment.

Transport and geographical barriers

Water locations are often outside cities, and public transport does not always provide convenient access.

Possible solutions:

  • Organising a group transfer from an accessible transport hub.
  • Coordinating shared journeys through local youth and scout organisations.
  • Choosing locations with accessible public transport.
  • Taking into account the needs of participants with mobility impairments.
  • Planning arrival time with transfers, rest and extra travel time in mind.

Before the activity, check the actual route, the accessibility of stops, the distance to the shore and the possibility of a safe drop-off and return.

Language barriers

In international youth groups, participants may have different levels of the working language. It is especially important to make sure that safety instructions on the water are understood.

Possible solutions:

  • Using simple, short sentences.
  • Demonstrating actions instead of relying only on verbal explanation.
  • Preparing visual cards with basic commands.
  • Involving bilingual volunteers.
  • Allowing extra time for questions and repetition.
  • Checking understanding by having people show the action, not only by asking "Is everything clear?".

Practical example: before a windsurfing session, the instructor demonstrates on the shore the body position, handling of the sail and stop signals. Participants repeat the actions on land before moving on to practice on the water.

Critical safety signals must be equally clear to all participants regardless of language.

Physical accessibility and individual needs

Not all participants can move along the shore, use standard equipment, keep their balance or perform physically demanding tasks equally easily.

Possible solutions:

  • Choosing locations with accessible routes, sanitary facilities and a safe approach to the water.
  • Using adapted boats and equipment where available.
  • Assigning tasks by function rather than by physical ability.
  • Involving instructors with experience in adaptive water sports.
  • Introducing shorter practical sessions and additional breaks.
  • Offering alternative roles: navigator, wind observer, team coordinator, equipment preparation assistant.

Important: an alternative role must not automatically replace time on the water for a person with a disability. First find out their wishes and, together with the instructor, assess the possibilities for safe adaptation.

Social barriers and group relationships

Young people may feel unsure because of lack of experience, language differences, social isolation, fear of making mistakes or a negative experience in a group.

Possible solutions:

  • Forming mixed teams with different levels of experience.
  • Introducing a mutual support system (buddy system).
  • Rotating roles regularly so that every participant can try different tasks.
  • Not allowing mockery of mistakes, discrimination or unwanted pressure.
  • Recognising individual progress, not only sporting results.

The leader should make sure that more experienced participants do not monopolise steering the boat or decision-making, and that beginners do not remain passive observers.

3. Supporting young people who have lived through war or forced displacement

Young people who have experienced war, loss of home, separation from loved ones or forced displacement may need extra support during group activities.

At the same time, do not assume that every participant with such an experience has psychological difficulties or needs special treatment.

The approach should be based on the principles of trauma-informed youth work — youth work that takes into account possible traumatic experience.

Core principles

1. Predictability and a sense of safety

Before the activity, explain to participants the programme, the length of sessions, the expected physical load, the safety rules and possible changes of plan.

Pay particular attention to sound signals, unexpected commands, physical contact and situations that may cause discomfort.

2. Right to choose

A participant can choose between several available tasks, ask for a break or decline a specific exercise without having to explain personal reasons.

Declining to go out on the water does not mean being excluded from the learning process.

3. No pressure to talk about personal experience

Do not require young people to talk about war, losses, displacement or other difficult events. Reflection should focus on learning, cooperation, new skills and feelings during the activity.

4. Gradual involvement

Participants can be offered several levels of participation:

  • Observation
  • Getting to know the equipment, the rules and the work of the team on the shore.
  • Participation with support
  • Doing simple tasks together with the leader or a partner.
  • Practice on the water
  • Doing adapted exercises on the water after the necessary briefing and a check of safety conditions.

These levels are not a compulsory sequence: a participant can change the form of participation according to their own readiness.

What the leader does in case of strong emotional distress

If a participant feels panic, strong fear or emotional overload:

1. Stop the task concerned and ensure physical safety. 2. Calmly offer to move to a quieter, safe place. 3. Ask what help is needed, without insisting on explanations. 4. Offer water, rest, the presence of a trusted person or other available support. 5. If needed, contact the designated safeguarding officer. 6. In case of a serious threat to health or safety, act according to the emergency response plan.

The leader must not carry out psychological therapy on their own or demand discussion of traumatic events. If necessary, the participant is referred to qualified specialists.

4. Group agreements and psychological safety

A psychologically safe environment is created through clear rules, consistent behaviour of leaders and the possibility to speak openly about needs and discomfort.

At the start of the programme the group works out short agreements together.

Example of group agreements

Our Safe Crew — the rules of our team

1. We respect different experience, abilities and learning pace. 2. We do not mock mistakes, fears or language difficulties. 3. Everyone has the right to ask for help or a break. 4. We do not touch another person or help physically without their consent, except for necessary actions in an emergency. 5. We do not force others to share personal stories. 6. We tell the instructor about a dangerous situation or discomfort. 7. We support each other and recognise even small achievements.

Present the agreements in clear language and, where possible, illustrate them with pictograms. They should apply to participants as well as to leaders and instructors.

How to ask for help

Before sessions begin, every participant should know who is responsible for safety, whom they can tell about a problem and how to do it confidentially.

1. A participant feels discomfort or notices a problem

Fear, conflict, discrimination, unsafe behaviour

2. They tell the leader or the designated trusted person

In person, through an agreed channel or with the help of a volunteer

3. The responsible person assesses the situation

Listens, ensures safety, explains the next steps

4. The necessary support is organised

Adapting the activity, resolving the situation or involving specialists

5. Check whether the participant feels safe

Without pressure and without publicly discussing their personal situation

If the complaint concerns the leader personally, the participant must have an alternative way to report directly to the safeguarding officer or the project coordinator.

Information should be passed on only to those who need it to protect the participant. Absolute confidentiality must not be promised, especially if there is a threat to a person's safety.

For minors, the procedure for involving parents or legal representatives must be defined in advance, in line with current child protection rules and the nature of the situation.

5. Practical application during water sessions

The recommendations below should be built directly into the sailing and windsurfing teaching scenarios.

Scenario 1. The first time on the water

A participant is afraid to go out in a boat because they have no experience.

Adaptation: first get to know the boat on the shore, then a demonstration and a short practice with the instructor in favourable conditions.

If the participant is not ready to go out on the water, they can help with navigation tasks or observing the wind.

Scenario 2. Learning windsurfing in an international group

Some participants do not understand all of the instructor's spoken commands.

Adaptation: short instructions, visual cards, demonstrating movements on land, repeating safety signals and the support of a bilingual volunteer.

Scenario 3. A participant temporarily does not take part in water practice

Because of tiredness, emotional discomfort or other reasons, a participant decides to stay on the shore.

Adaptation: offer a meaningful alternative — analysing weather conditions, keeping an observation log, preparing a route or helping with learning materials.

The participant remains part of the team and takes part in the shared reflection.

6. Reflection and assessing inclusiveness

After a session it is important to assess not only how technical skills were learned, but also the experience of participation, cooperation and the sense of safety.

Reflection can be done orally in a circle, in pairs, through short written answers or anonymous notes. Participants have the right to skip a question.

Examples of questions in the Youthpass approach:

  • What new thing did I learn to do today?
  • At what moment did I feel the support of the team?
  • Was I able to choose a comfortable way to take part?
  • What helped me feel more confident?
  • Which cooperation skill can I use in other situations?
  • What could the organisers change to make the next session more accessible?

The answers are used to improve future activities. Personal stories, medical information and other sensitive data are not included in a public report without proper grounds and consent.

7. Recommendations for organisers before the activity

Leader's checklist

0 of 8

1. Possible financial, transport, language and physical barriers identified 2. Accessibility of the location, sanitary facilities and approach to the water checked 3. Necessary equipment and instructors' competence confirmed 4. Safety rules explained to participants in clear language 5. Alternative meaningful roles and the possibility of a break provided 6. Safeguarding officer and an alternative way to report designated 7. Emergency response plan agreed 8. Accessible forms of reflection prepared

8. Expected results

Applying these recommendations will help to:

  • Reduce financial, social, language and physical barriers to taking part in water activities.
  • Ensure meaningful involvement of young people with different abilities and levels of experience.
  • Support young people who have lived through war or forced displacement, without stigma or pressure.
  • Create a group environment based on mutual respect, trust and psychological safety.
  • Develop skills of cooperation, communication, responsibility and independence.
  • Give leaders practical tools for adapting water sessions to the needs of local youth communities.

In summary: inclusive youth work in water sports is not only about the physical accessibility of boats, equipment or the shore. It is about creating conditions in which every young person can learn, cooperate, make decisions and feel like a full member of the team.
