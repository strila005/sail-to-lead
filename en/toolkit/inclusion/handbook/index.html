INTRODUCTION

This booklet helps a leader run a water activity with an educational purpose: set goals, organise reflection and help participants write down their learning outcomes. It is intended for leaders who work with young people aged 11–18, including those who have little experience with water or fewer opportunities to take part.

How to use it: Chapter 5 can be run without any other materials. The texts for the leader are written so that they can be read aloud. The examples of children's answers are guides; they do not need to be reproduced word for word.

What you need for every session:

  • The participant booklet (separate, see the corresponding file).
  • Pens or pencils.
  • Stickers or cards in two colours (for the scale and for group answers), or a sheet with a 1–5 scale on a board or on the shore.
  • A timer.

CHAPTER 1. LEARNING GOALS

1.1. What is a learning goal

A learning goal describes what a participant should learn or be able to do after the session. The session programme says what we do. The goal says why, and how we will know that the result has been reached.

1.2. How to formulate a goal

A goal has three parts:

  • Action: what exactly the participant will do or understand ("turn the sailing boat around on their own").
  • Criterion: how the participant and the leader will see that the goal has been reached ("twice in a row without the instructor's help").
  • Conditions: for whom and in what conditions ("in shallow water, in light wind").

Examples for sailing:

  • "I understand how to change course by giving the crew a command, and I do it without prompting."
  • "I can name 3 parts of a sailing boat and explain what each is for."

Examples for windsurfing:

  • "I hold my stance on the board for 10 seconds in steady wind."
  • "I turn the board from one tack to the other on the first attempt, or I explain what is getting in the way."

1.3. Goals for different levels

  • Beginners: one action per session, a simple criterion, a lot of practice.
  • Experienced participants: a goal about behaviour in the team or a technical task (choosing a route with the forecast in mind).
  • Participants with fewer opportunities: the goal is chosen together with the participant, relative to their own starting point. "Sit in the boat and handle the sheet" is just as complete a goal.

1.4. Mistakes to avoid

  • A goal that cannot be checked ("feel the wind").
  • A goal that compares participants with each other.
  • More than one goal per session.
  • A goal imposed without discussion. The participant chooses from two or three options.

CHAPTER 2. KEY COMPETENCES AND YOUTHPASS

2.1. What is a competence

A competence is a combination of knowledge, skills and attitudes. It is developed through practice and reflection. This pack relies on the eight key competences in the 2018 version, as described in the Youthpass tool.

2.2. The eight competences and how they show on the water

CompetenceHow it shows on the water
1. Multilingual competenceUnderstanding commands and terms, communicating with participants from other countries.
2. Personal, social and learning to learn competenceManaging fear, cooperating in the crew, assessing one's own progress.
3. Citizenship competenceResponsibility for equipment, for the nature of the water body, for the rules of the site.
4. Entrepreneurship competencePlanning a route, taking initiative, decisions involving risk.
5. Cultural awareness and expressionCreative presentation (a crew name, a video recording).
6. Digital competenceReading a wind forecast, using apps safely, filming video.
7. Mathematical competence and STEMWind direction and strength, angles, the physics of sailing.
CompetenceHow it shows on the water
8. Literacy competenceUnderstanding instructions, describing experience in writing and speech.

2.3. How to choose competences for a session

  • Choose 1–3 competences that match the goal of the session.
  • For sailing, the personal, social and learning to learn and the citizenship competences often come into play naturally.
  • For windsurfing, the personal, social and learning to learn and the mathematical/STEM competences often come into play naturally.
  • Competences that were not at the centre of the session are not filled in.

2.4. Link with Youthpass

  • Youthpass recognises non-formal learning and records the competences a participant has developed.
  • Not every participant will have every competence.
  • Specific skills that do not fit into the eight are written in the "Other specific skills" or "Task-Specific Competences" field, depending on the type of certificate.
  • Youthpass is a separate outcome for each participant, not part of the group's reflection.

CHAPTER 3. REFLECTION

3.1. What is reflection

Reflection is calm thinking over an experience: what happened, what the participant felt, what they will do next. Without reflection a session remains just an activity.

3.2. Three basic questions

1. What happened? (facts, no judgements) 2. What did I feel and why? 3. What will I do the same or differently next time?

For ages 11–14: "What worked? What was hard? What will I try next time?"

3.3. Reflection formats

Individual (5–10 min): a written or drawn note in the booklet. Confidence can be rated on a 1–5 scale at the start and at the end of the session.

In pairs (10 min): participants take turns asking each other two questions. The partner writes down one thought from the person they talked to.

Group (15–20 min): a circle after the session, each person names one thing that worked and one thing that was hard. Everyone has the right to pass. A 1–5 scale with stickers.

3.4. The leader's role

  • Ask questions and wait for answers; do not fill the pauses.
  • Do not judge the answers, accept them.
  • Notice who is silent and offer an alternative (writing, drawing, the scale).
  • Do not ask people to share personal things: health, family, stories of war or displacement. If a participant shares them on their own, they are listened to, but the topic is not developed without their consent.

3.5. Safety during reflection

  • The group rules are spoken aloud before starting: respect another person's opinion, the right not to answer, the right to say you are uncomfortable.
  • If a strong emotion appears, first shift attention to safety and wellbeing, then talk to the participant separately. The responsible person and the safety rules are in a separate section of the pack.

3.6. Time

15–20 minutes after the session. Reflection should not be longer than the session itself.

CHAPTER 4. WRITING LEARNING OUTCOMES

4.1. Template

Formula for a learning outcome: "I learned [action] [in which situation] [what it changed or what I can do now]."

4.2. Examples for ages 11–14

  • "I can ask for help when I am scared on the water." (Personal, social and learning to learn)
  • "I can name 3 parts of a sailing boat and explain their role." (Literacy / STEM)
  • "Together with the crew I made a plan which we carried out." (Personal, social and learning to learn)

4.3. Examples for ages 15–18

  • "I set myself a goal for the session, define a criterion of success and check whether I have reached it." (Personal, social and learning to learn)
  • "I compared my confidence on the water over three sessions and can explain what influenced it." (Personal, social and learning to learn)
  • "I suggested an idea to the crew and got it across to the others." (Entrepreneurship)
  • "I used the wind forecast to choose a route and explained my choice." (Digital / STEM)

4.4. Participants with fewer opportunities

The structure is the same, but progress is compared with the participant's own starting point: "I got to [outcome] compared with the beginning." Do not compare with others.

4.5. Typical mistakes

  • General wording without an action: "I learned to be a team player."
  • A judgement instead of a description: "I was very good."
  • Wording that the leader wrote for the participant. The participant writes their own version; the leader helps to clarify the words but does not change the meaning.

CHAPTER 5. READY-MADE SESSIONS TO RUN

Each session contains: the purpose, the time, what is needed, a step-by-step text for the leader, examples of children's answers, and what to do if children are silent or answer briefly.

SESSION A. SETTING GOALS AS A GROUP (at the start of a session or week)

Purpose: each participant chooses one learning goal for the session and writes it down. Time: 15 minutes. What you need: the participant booklet (page "My goal for the session"), pens, a board or a sheet with three examples of goals.

Step 1. Introduction (1 min)

Text for the leader (read aloud): "Today we are going out on the water. Before we start, each of you will choose one small goal. A goal is something you want to learn or do. It has to be concrete so that you can see for yourselves that you have reached it. It is not a grade, it is your plan."

Step 2. Examples (3 min)

The leader shows three examples of different difficulty:

  • "Today I will stand up on the board on my own, without help."
  • "Today I will give the crew one command and check that everyone understood it."
  • "Today for the first time I will ask the instructor what to do when the boat tilts."

Step 3. Choosing (5 min)

Text for the leader (read aloud): "Choose one goal from these or think up your own. Write in the booklet: what I want to do, how I will know it worked, and what small step I will take first. If you do not know what to choose, write the smallest goal that you think is realistic."

Step 4. Discussion in pairs (5 min)

Participants take turns reading their goal to a partner. The partner asks one question: "How will you know you have achieved it?" If the answer is unclear, it can be clarified.

Examples of children's answers (ages 11–14):

  • "I want to get into the boat on my own and push off from the shore. I will know I am holding the paddle if the boat moves without me."
  • "I want not to get scared when the boat tilts. I will know if I tell the instructor and do not hang on to the side."
  • "I want to remember 3 parts of a sailing boat. I will know if I name them without prompts."

Examples of children's answers (ages 15–18):

  • "My goal is to sail one tack without help. I will check whether I hold the course myself and whether the team reacts to my commands."
  • "I want to try the role of helmsman and make decisions about the course. I will know if the crew carried out my plan without comments."

If the children are silent: The leader offers a choice of three options and says the goal can be changed after the first step. If a participant sets a goal that is clearly dangerous, the leader explains why and together they choose a safer option.

Adaptation: For participants with fewer opportunities the goal is discussed separately, relative to their own starting point. For example: "Today I want to hold the sheet with both hands and say when it is comfortable for me." The goal can be "sit in the boat and see the shores", and that is a complete result.

Closing (1 min)

Leader: "Your goals stay in the booklet. At the end of the session we will come back to them."

SESSION B. REFLECTION AFTER A WATER SESSION

Purpose: participants discuss what happened in the session, what worked and what was hard. Time: 20 minutes. What you need: the participant booklet (page "Reflection after the session"), stickers or cards, a 1–5 scale on a board.

Step 1. Silence and the scale (2 min)

Text for the leader (read aloud): "Now everyone will think and mark in the booklet how confident they feel after the session. The scale goes from 1 to 5, where 1 is not at all confident and 5 is very confident."

Step 2. Individual note (5 min)

The leader shows three questions written on the board:

  • What happened?
  • What did I feel and why?
  • What will I do the same or differently next time?

Text for the leader (read aloud): "Answer in words, in a drawing or in one word. Nobody will check."

Step 3. Conversation in pairs (6 min)

Text for the leader (read aloud): "Find a partner. One asks the second question from the board, the other answers. Then you swap. The partner writes down one thought from the person they talked to."

Step 4. Circle (7 min)

The leader asks around the circle: "One thing that worked. One thing that was hard." A participant may say "pass". The leader does not comment on every answer, but sums up at the end.

Examples of children's answers (ages 11–14):

  • "What worked: I pushed off from the shore on my own. What was hard: I was scared when the boat tilted." (About sailing)
  • "What worked: I am not afraid of falling off the board any more. What was hard: keeping my balance for a long time." (About windsurfing)
  • "What worked: I knew the command in English. What was hard: the instructor spoke fast."
  • "Next time I will try asking them to explain it again."

Examples of children's answers (ages 15–18):

  • "I thought I would be scared the whole time, but after the second tack the fear got smaller. Next time I want to choose the route myself."
  • "In the crew I listened to others more than I spoke. That was good, but I want to express my opinion more often."
  • "The hardest thing was not getting angry when the wind changed. Next time I will return to the plan more calmly."

If the children are silent: The leader offers a choice (a drawing, one word, the scale) and says they can answer later in writing.

Adaptations: For younger children: instead of three questions, give two: "What worked?" and "What was hard?" Mood scale: 😟 😐 🙂 😀 🤩. For older participants: add the question "What can I apply in another situation: school, family, community?"

Questions by type of water session:

  • Sailing: "Which role in the crew was the most comfortable, and which would you like to try?"; "What helped you not to be afraid when the boat tilted? Whom did you ask for help?"; "How did the crew make decisions? What did you change in your behaviour in the team?"
  • Windsurfing: "Which moment was the hardest? What did you do to make it a little easier?"; "How do you feel now compared with the first session?"; "What next small goal are you setting?"

Closing (2 min)

Leader: "Thank you. Your answers stay in the booklet. Next time we will come back to the goal you chose today."

SESSION C. REFLECTION BY COMPETENCES

Purpose: participants understand which competences they developed during the session and can describe it in their own words. Time: 25 minutes. What you need: the participant booklet (page "My competences"), cards with the names of the competences (in Ukrainian, with the English in brackets), pens.

Step 1. Explanation (3 min)

Text for the leader (read aloud): "A competence is what you can do, what you know and how you feel about it, all together. Today we will look at which of them you used on the water. You do not need to choose all of them. Choose 1–3 that you noticed in yourself."

Step 2. Cards (3 min)

The leader shows eight cards with short descriptions:

  • 1. Multilingual: understanding commands and communicating.
  • 2. Personal, social and learning to learn: fear, team, assessing progress.
  • 3. Citizenship: responsibility for equipment and nature.
  • 4. Entrepreneurship: initiative, plan, decisions.
  • 5. Cultural awareness and expression: creativity, crew name, rituals.
  • 6. Digital: forecast, video, apps.
  • 7. Mathematical, STEM: wind direction, angle, physics.
  • 8. Literacy: instructions, description, conversation.

Step 3. Choosing and explaining (7 min)

Text for the leader (read aloud): "Choose 1–3 cards that best fit what you did. Then write one sentence: 'I used ... because ...'. For example: 'I used the instructor's commands because I understood what was needed from me.'"

Examples of children's answers (ages 11–14):

  • "I chose personal and social, because I asked for help when I was scared."
  • "I chose language, because I understood the instructor's command even though they were not speaking Ukrainian."
  • "I chose mathematical, because I worked out where the wind blows from and turned the boat the right way."

Examples of children's answers (ages 15–18):

  • "I chose entrepreneurship and personal. I suggested a new route even though it was risky, and it worked."
  • "I chose citizenship, because I took responsibility for the equipment and checked that everyone had closed their straps."
  • "I chose digital, because I looked at the wind forecast before going out and it affected my choice of board."

Step 4. Pair or circle (8 min)

Participants take turns reading their sentence to a partner or the circle. The partner or the leader asks: "How did you notice that?" The question helps to clarify the evidence.

Step 5. Writing in the Youthpass way (4 min)

Text for the leader (read aloud): "Your sentence is a future learning outcome. It can be rewritten in the formula: 'I learned [action] [in which situation] [what it changed].' If the sentence sounds general, add the situation or an example."

Example of rewriting:

  • Before: "I was a team person."
  • After: "I learned to share duties on the boat when there were four of us, and now I can suggest a split at the start."

Adaptation: If a child finds it hard to choose a card, the leader can name three options from the session and ask which is closer. They can answer with a drawing or a gesture.

Closing (2 min)

Leader: "These sentences go into your booklet and into Youthpass if you choose to have one. You can change them at any time."

SESSION D. FINAL REVIEW OF GOALS

Purpose: participants compare their initial goal with the result, formulate a summary and the next small goal. Time: 20 minutes. What you need: the participant booklet (pages "My goal for the session", "Summary of one session"), pens, a 1–5 scale.

Step 1. Back to the goal (3 min)

Text for the leader (read aloud): "Open the page where you wrote your goal for the session. Read it to yourself."

Step 2. Was it achieved (5 min)

The participant answers three questions:

  • Did I achieve the goal? Fully, partly, not at all?
  • What is the evidence? What exactly did I see, or what did someone notice?
  • What helped and what got in the way?

Text for the leader (read aloud): "If the goal was not achieved, that is also a result. Now you will find out what gets in the way, and this will help next time."

Examples of children's answers (ages 11–14):

  • "Goal: push off from the shore on my own. Achieved fully. Evidence: the instructor said I did it without help. What helped: I waited until the wind became steady."
  • "Goal: not to get scared when the boat tilts. Achieved partly. Evidence: I held on to the side but did not scream. What got in the way: it was tilted very far."

Examples of children's answers (ages 15–18):

  • "Goal: sail a tack without help. Achieved partly. Evidence: on the first tack the instructor prompted me, on the second no longer. What helped: practice from the first session. What got in the way: a bad forecast, which I had not checked."
  • "Goal: make a decision about the course. Achieved fully. Evidence: the crew agreed with my route. What helped: that I talked to everyone beforehand."

Step 3. The scale (2 min)

Text for the leader (read aloud): "Now rate your confidence on the water from 1 to 5, as you did at the start of the session. Compare: what has changed?"

Step 4. A summary in one sentence (5 min)

Text for the leader (read aloud): "Write one sentence: 'Today I learned ...' This is your main result of this session." You can use the template from Chapter 4.

  • "Today I learned not to be afraid of the command when the boat is going fast."
  • "Today I learned to hold the course without the instructor shouting."

Examples of children's answers (ages 15–18):

  • "Today I learned to make decisions on the boat and to check that the others understood them."
  • "Today I learned to plan a session according to the forecast and explain my choice to the team."

Step 5. The next small goal (3 min)

Text for the leader (read aloud): "Write one next goal, a small one, for the next session. It should be something that can be checked." Examples: "Next time I will check the forecast myself before going out", "Next time I will say the command loudly, and everyone will hear me."

Step 6. Closing as a group (2 min)

Participants name one word about today in a circle. The leader does not comment but says thank you.

If the children are silent: Offer the option of answering in writing or in a drawing; ask what they remember best.

Adaptation: For participants with fewer opportunities the goal and the evidence can be visual (a photo or a drawing they choose themselves). If a participant does not want to share, the scale is enough.

Closing (1 min)

Leader: "Your summaries are your personal result. You can show them to the instructor or the Youthpass coordinator if you want."

CHAPTER 6. ADAPTATIONS FOR YOUNG PEOPLE WITH DIFFERENT ABILITIES

  • Pace: give more time to answer, do not interrupt.
  • Form of the answer: a word, a drawing, the scale, in writing, by voice or by gesture.
  • Goal: chosen taking the starting point into account and can be "accessible".
  • Silence: do not treat silence as refusal. Ask in writing, later.
  • Language: explain terms in simple words, and if needed in two languages.
  • Safety: before every session check that the participant knows the responsible person.
  • Further guidance is in a separate section of the pack (safety and equipment adaptation).
