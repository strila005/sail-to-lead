Eco-camp guidelines - practical recommendations for eco-friendly camping "Leave No Trace" is a principle of responsible time in nature, the aim of which is to minimise a person's negative impact on the environment. Its main idea is that after a rest stop or camp is over, the natural area should stay as close as possible to how it was before the group arrived. For water and coastal camps this principle is especially important, because people's activity can affect not only the shore but also the quality of the water, the vegetation and the aquatic and animal life. Before the camp starts, the organisers must familiarise themselves with the local rules, the conservation status of the area and the restrictions on using the water body. If the area belongs to a protected zone, it is necessary to check in advance the permitted places for camping, swimming, lighting fires, fishing, boating and other water activities. The group must use only officially permitted places and must not create new paths, camp spots or fire sites. Special attention should be paid to waste. Camp participants must have access to containers for sorting rubbish and follow the local rules for disposing of it. Do not leave packaging, plastic bottles, food scraps, ropes or other equipment on the shore. If there are no containers in a particular place, the rubbish must be taken away and disposed of in a suitable place. It is also worth minimising single-use plastic and using reusable bottles, tableware and food containers. During water activities, pollution of the water body must be prevented. Do not wash dishes, equipment or yourself with soap or other detergents directly in the water body unless the rules of the specific location allow it. Fuel, chemicals and other pollutants must be stored in a way that rules out their getting into the water. Participants must also keep to the designated areas for swimming and boating and not approach places where this could damage natural habitats. Respect for wildlife is another important element of responsible camping. Do not chase, feed or deliberately disturb wild animals and birds. Avoid noise near their nesting, breeding or resting places. Plants, reeds, aquatic plants, trees and other natural elements should not be damaged or used to set up the camp. Equipment should be set up so that it does not destroy the plant cover or block animals' natural routes. A fire may be lit only in specially designated places and only when this is permitted by local rules and the weather. The fire must be completely put out after use and no hot embers or other dangerous materials may be left. In periods of heightened fire danger, lighting fires may be banned altogether. It is also important to use natural resources economically. Participants must not leave water running without need, must switch off lights and electrical appliances after use and must not use more resources than necessary. For moving around a natural area it is preferable to use existing paths and roads, and for water activities — designated routes and launch points for boats or other equipment. Before the end of the camp, the organisers must carry out a final check of the area: collect all rubbish and check the camp spots, fire sites, the shoreline and the places where activities were held. All equipment, ropes, packaging and other items brought by the group must be taken away. If possible, hold a short "eco-check" together with the participants so that they can see for themselves that no traces of the camp are left. Eco-friendly camping is responsible use of space in which participants take into account the impact of their actions on the water, the shore, plants, animals and other visitors.

Practical application of ecological principles

Environmental responsibility must accompany every stage of the camp. When choosing a site, the organiser checks the conservation status of the area, the permitted places for camping, access to water and possible restrictions on water activities. When setting up the camp, the group uses only designated places, does not damage vegetation, minimises waste and organises its collection properly. During water sessions, participants use permitted routes and launch points, do not disturb animals and do not allow the water body to be polluted. After the camp ends, the area is checked: all rubbish and equipment is taken away, the camp spots and activity areas are left clean, and the group makes sure that its stay has left no noticeable negative impact. It is also necessary to distinguish general ecological principles from the local rules of a specific area. The principles of "Leave No Trace" and responsible use of nature can be applied as a general basis for any camp, but the specific rules on camping, fire, swimming, boating, routes, access to the shore or animal protection may differ from place to place. Before holding a camp, the organiser must check the current requirements with the administration of the protected area, the local operator or another responsible body and communicate these rules to all participants.

Environmental responsibility in water sessions and camp programmes

Environmental responsibility should be included not only as a separate theoretical part of the camp but as a constant principle for organising all water sessions and the group's daily life. Participants must understand that the water body, the shore and the surrounding area are part of a natural ecosystem, and water sport must take place in such a way as to minimise disturbance to it.

Before water activities begin, it is advisable to hold a short eco-briefing. In it participants are told the rules of conduct on the water and the shore: do not leave rubbish, do not damage vegetation, do not disturb animals, use only permitted places for launching boats and swimming and observe the restrictions of the specific natural area. For young people these rules can be combined with a practical task — for example, marking conservation zones on a map, finding places for safe access to the water or carrying out a short "eco-check" after a session.

During SUP, kayaking, sailing and other water activities, instructors can draw participants' attention to the surroundings: birds, aquatic plants, shore ecosystems and changes in water level. At the same time, watching nature must not turn into disturbing it. For example, do not approach bird nesting places or chase animals to take photos. It is especially important to keep to the established routes and restrictions in protected areas.

Environmental responsibility can also be part of the teaching methodology. After a water session the group can briefly discuss how their activity affected the environment, what could have been done better and what natural features the participants saw. For older participants, simple practical tasks can be organised: bird watching, photographing natural features without damaging them, mapping rubbish on the shoreline or cleaning up a small stretch of shore.

It is also important to link environmental education with responsible consumption at camp. The group can use reusable bottles and tableware, minimise single-use plastic, sort waste and use water and electricity economically. In this way environmental responsibility becomes part of everyday behaviour and not just a rule during water sessions.

Environmental practices of Zeeland

Zeeland is a particularly suitable example for this approach, since a large part of its natural environment is connected with the sea, delta waters, shores, dunes, tidal areas and other aquatic ecosystems. The province has a separate system of protected areas — the Natuurnetwerk Zeeland, which includes dunes, delta waters, salt marshes, creeks, dykes, meadows and forests, among others.

An important principle of Zeeland is combining recreation with nature conservation. For example, the Veerse Meer is used at the same time for water sports, tourism and leisure, but the development of the area is planned with water quality, nature, landscape and safety in mind. This can be a good model for a youth camp: water activities must not rule out conservation goals but should be organised so as to coexist with them.

Natura 2000 areas are of special importance. In Zeeland they include, among others, the Oosterschelde, Grevelingen, Veerse Meer, Voordelta and Westerschelde & Saeftinghe. Special rules and permit requirements may apply to activity that could affect protected species or areas. So before holding a camp the organisers must check the current rules for the specific location.

A good example of conservation practice is Waterdunen — an area that combines coastal protection, conservation work and recreation. Conditions have been created for bird watching, and special viewing structures allow visitors to watch the birds without disturbing their peace. This approach can be transferred directly into a youth programme: instead of approaching animals, participants watch them from designated places and learn to understand their environment.

For the Grevelingenmeer, which is a large saltwater lake and a popular water sports centre, a combination of recreational use and natural value is characteristic. On its shores you can see birds, wild animals and seals. So water sessions in such an environment can serve both a sporting and an educational function — for example, by including nature observation and a short discussion of how water tourism can affect the ecosystem.

For the shared public pack

On the basis of these principles, a short "Eco-camp code" can be included in the public pack for participants:

  1. Plan responsibly — check the rules of the specific water body and protected area.
  2. Use permitted places — do not create new camp spots, paths or boat launch points.
  3. Do not disturb nature — watch animals from a safe distance and do not enter prohibited zones.
  4. Leave it clean — everything the group brought to the shore or the water must be taken back.
  5. Minimise waste — use reusable equipment and sort rubbish.
  6. Protect the water — do not let detergents, fuel or other pollution get into the water body.
  7. Learn through practice — observe nature and discuss the impact of human activity.
  8. Leave nature for those who come next — the main principle: after the camp the area should remain the same as or better than it was before it began.

4. Community experience

Brouwersdam — our own experience. We held a camp here as part of the Erasmus+ project "Sail to Lead" together with Plast. The participants liked it very much, and the organisers fully trust the water safety. The date of the camp needs to be filled in. This is the experience of our group, not guaranteed operator terms: the contents of the package and the prices still need to be confirmed.

Google reviews (as of 13.11.2026): 4.5 ★ (1,220 reviews). Guests praise the friendly staff, patient instructors and clean, compact lodges with a good breakfast. Some find the food choice and the atmosphere in the restaurant average.

De Kaag — needs a preliminary check. There is no own experience or reviews from local organisations yet. Before choosing it is worth:

  • talking to local scout groups (Scouting Nederland, especially water scouts) and CWO instructors who have worked with De Kaag;
  • asking the operator about water safety procedures, the instructors' qualifications and the ratio of instructors to participants;
  • checking the safety of the site with the "Safety and risk management" group;
  • if possible, visiting the place in advance.

Google reviews (as of 13.11.2026): 4.6 ★ (73 reviews). Reviews praise strong teachers (you can learn to sail from scratch), children's camps and corporate events. There are few reviews, so the rating is less reliable.

2. Example calculation for one group

Conditions: 20 people, 3 nights, accommodation only, without meals and parking. For another scenario the calculation must be repeated.

  • HOM Puck, 10 double rooms in season: 10 × 260 × 3 = 7,800 zł (130 zł per person per night). Off season: 10 × 200 × 3 = 6,000 zł.
  • Ukta, 5 "Brda" houses for 4 people: 5 × 380 × 3 = 5,700 zł (95 zł per person per night), a "from" price. Climate tax extra: 20 × 3 × 2.50 = 150 zł.
  • Ukta, tent field: 20 × 3 × 30 = 1,800 zł (30 zł per person per night) and also a climate tax of 150 zł.
  • Ukta, three meals a day: 20 × 3 × 90 = 5,400 zł for all three days, for adults. For HOM Puck the price of meals is unknown.

Without full board and without tents at HOM Puck a direct price comparison is not possible: per night, the Ukta houses are cheaper, and the tents are several times cheaper.

3. Brief summary

HOM Puck: the sea, hotel comfort, year-round operation, ecological programmes on yachts. This is our assessment based on the data collected.

PTTK Ukta: a river and forest, houses and tents, kayaks on the Krutynia, full group organisation of camps and a clear price list. It has no sailing fleet, only paddling and river tourism.

2. Wartime requirements for children's camps

These are requirements for Ukrainian children's recreation facilities. Whether they apply to an international youth camp depends on how it is registered. Clarify with the operator and with the State Service for Food Safety and Consumer Protection.

  • A shelter is one of the main conditions for a camp to operate. No more children may be admitted than the design capacity allows.
  • Admission only with a medical certificate on form 079/o, the staff undergo a medical examination.
  • No more than 5 people in rooms for younger children and no more than 4 for older ones, each child has a separate bed.
  • Meals four or five times a day, food not from the camp is prohibited.
  • For every 100 children one drinking fountain or bottled water. Water quality is checked before every season.
  • For tent camps the State Service for Food Safety and Consumer Protection issued separate explanations in 2026: where the camp may be located, medical assistance, fire safety. They must be read before choosing a base.
